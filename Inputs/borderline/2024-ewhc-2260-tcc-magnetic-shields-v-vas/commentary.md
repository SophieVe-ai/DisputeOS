# Magnetic Shields Ltd v Vacuum and Atmosphere Services Ltd — [2024] EWHC 2260 (TCC)
- **Court / venue:** High Court of Justice, Business and Property Courts of England and Wales, Technology and Construction Court (KBD), Royal Courts of Justice, Rolls Building, London EC4A 1NL (Case No HT-2022-000079)
- **Judge:** Jason Coppel KC (sitting as a Deputy High Court Judge)
- **Hearing / hand-down:** Hearing 14-16 and 20 May, 10 June and 2 September 2024; judgment 2 September 2024
- **Source:** https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/tcc/2024/2260
- **Tier:** borderline; mainly a customer's defective-works damages claim. The invoice element is small: the contractor's counterclaims on two unpaid invoices (GBP 2,196.78 and GBP 902.40 gross). Useful on which party's terms govern interest when the invoice is sent after performance, and on contractual set-off of unliquidated damages against invoices.

- **Creditor on the invoices (Defendant / counterclaimant):** Vacuum and Atmosphere Services Ltd ("VAS"), the contractor that refurbished vacuum furnaces and supplied parts [para 2].
- **Debtor on the invoices (Claimant):** Magnetic Shields Ltd ("MSL"), the furnace owner/customer [para 2].

### Amounts
- Counterclaims (unpaid invoices): GBP 2,196.78 and GBP 902.40 gross; GBP 1,830.65 and GBP 752 net of VAT [paras 55-56, 58].
- Debtor's damages claim against creditor: GBP 12,850 (VFS repairs) up to GBP 199,015 (full refurbishment cost), plus GBP 78,543.17 additional costs, including GBP 57,608.17 for running other furnaces, GBP 21,295.12 water-system costs, GBP 19,335 transport/installation and GBP 1,600 for the VFS report [paras 7-8, 51, 53].
- Interest: contractual. VAS argued for its own invoice terms; MSL's terms give 2% above base rate [para 55].

### Debtor's defences and complaints
1. **Set-off of damages for defective refurbishment** of the Abar under MSL's terms §8.8. Defects were revealed by the overpressure incident of 15 Jun 2021 and the VFS report of 3 Sep 2021; proceedings were issued 14 Mar 2022 [paras 4-5, 7, 57].
2. **Invoice 18908 should be free warranty work** on the CVE furnace [para 56].
3. **Interest under MSL's terms, not VAS's**: the invoices referring to VAS terms were sent after delivery/performance (goods 17 Feb, invoice 22 Feb 2021; work 24 Mar, invoice 26 Mar 2021) [paras 55-56].
- Invoice 18751 was conceded in principle, subject to set-off [para 55].

## Outcome
### Decision on each defence
1. **Upheld.** §8.8 allowed MSL to set off any liability, liquidated or unliquidated, including under other contracts. MSL was therefore not liable to pay the invoices because VAS's breaches had already caused greater damage. In substance it had refuted liability after the incident, even without formally invoking set-off [para 57]. The breaches were established in part (commissioning to 0.8 bar instead of 5 bar, safety valve, water pressure switch, heat-exchanger switch, backfill valve, air-admit filter, drawings) [para 38]. Damages were assessed at GBP 15,000 reinstatement plus GBP 1,600 VFS report, giving GBP 16,600. The claims for the full refurbishment cost and the mitigation costs were rejected [paras 42-54].
2. **Rejected.** No evidence or argument that the work fell under warranty, and in any event that could not override the contract MSL had entered into [para 56].
3. **Upheld.** MSL's terms applied because the contracts were formed and performed before VAS's terms were mentioned. Interest runs at MSL's rate of 2% above base rate [paras 55-56].

### Money result
- VAS's counterclaims succeed: GBP 1,830.65 (invoice 18751) and GBP 752 (invoice 18908), net of VAT, totalling GBP 2,582.65 [para 58].
- These are set off against MSL's damages of GBP 16,600, leaving GBP 14,017.35 [paras 54, 58].
- Agreed contractual interest to MSL of GBP 2,217.62, "taking account of a small amount of interest payable to VAS" [para 59].
- Net judgment for MSL against VAS: GBP 16,234.97 [para 59]. Costs: not stated.

## Commentary
*(Commentary: author's analysis, not the court's.)*
- What decided the invoice issues was document sequence. The customer's purchase orders (with its terms) came first; the contractor's invoices carrying its own payment terms came only after delivery or performance and could not change the contract. The same reasoning defeated VAS's terms on the main contract [paras 13-14, 55-56].
- The invoices were valid debts and the "warranty work" objection failed. But a broad contractual set-off clause in the customer's terms meant the debts were absorbed by the larger damages claim, even though that claim was unliquidated and never formally asserted as a set-off.
- Lesson for collections: before pursuing a small invoice, check whose terms govern (look for a set-off clause in the buyer's PO terms) and whether the customer has a credible cross-claim on related work. Here pursuing about GBP 3,100 of invoices sat inside litigation that cost the contractor a net GBP 16,234.97.
- DisputeOS mapping: Evidence should date-stamp PO, delivery and invoice to decide which terms control. Reconciliation should calculate interest at the PO-terms rate (2% over base), not the invoice's "strictly 30 days" terms. Challenge should reject the warranty objection for lack of evidence, but uphold set-off where the debtor's terms permit set-off of unliquidated cross-claims and there is evidence of breach. Resolution should recommend a settlement or netting proposal rather than a court-claim pack, and flag that the creditor's own defective work exposes it to a larger damages claim.
